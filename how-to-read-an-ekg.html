How to read an EKG

An EKG is a graph of the heart's electrical activity over time. The trick to reading one is to never look at the whole thing at once. You walk through it in the same order, every time.

This is an educational overview of how EKGs are structured and interpreted. It is not medical advice and is not a substitute for formal clinical training. Real EKG interpretation belongs with a qualified clinician.
The Core Idea

Every heartbeat fires the same sequence: P wave (atria fire), QRS (ventricles fire), T wave (ventricles reset). Reading an EKG is checking whether that sequence is timed and shaped correctly.

01The waveform of one beat

One cardiac cycle. The green trace is what a monitor draws; the pink grid is standard EKG paper.

P WAVE

Atria depolarize

The atria contract and push blood into the ventricles. Should be small, smooth, and upright in lead II.

PR INTERVAL

The AV node pause

Time from atrial firing to ventricular firing. A deliberate delay that lets the ventricles fill before they squeeze.

Normal 120–200 ms
QRS COMPLEX

Ventricles depolarize

The big spike. The ventricles contract and eject blood. Narrow means the signal traveled the normal fast pathway.

Normal < 120 ms
ST SEGMENT

The flat stretch

Between contraction and reset. Should sit level with the baseline. Elevation or depression here is what flags ischemia.

T WAVE

Ventricles repolarize

The ventricles reset electrically for the next beat. Usually upright and rounded.

QT INTERVAL

Total ventricular time

Start of QRS to end of T. A long QT can predispose to dangerous rhythms, so it's always checked.

Corrected for rate (QTc)

03The systematic walk-through

Same order every time. This is what stops you from missing things.

Step 01 · Rate

How fast?

Divide 300 by the number of big boxes between two R waves. One box apart = 300 bpm, two = 150, three = 100, four = 75, five = 60, six = 50.

Normal 60–100 bpm
Step 02 · Rhythm

Regular or irregular?

Are the R waves evenly spaced? Is there a P wave before every QRS, and a QRS after every P? Even spacing with that pairing is normal sinus rhythm.

Step 03 · Axis

Which way is it pointing?

The net direction of the heart's electrical signal. Check leads I and aVF: both upright means a normal axis.

Normal −30° to +90°
Step 04 · Intervals

Are the timings right?

Measure PR, QRS, and QT by counting boxes. A long PR, a wide QRS, or a long QT each point to specific conduction problems.

Step 05 · Morphology

Do the waves look right?

Check the shape of P waves, QRS, and T waves. Tall, peaked, inverted, or notched shapes each carry meaning.

Step 06 · ST & T

Any ischemia?

Look at the ST segment and T waves across all leads. ST elevation or depression, and T inversion, are the classic signs of injury or ischemia.

04The 12 leads see different walls

Each lead is a camera angle on the heart. Grouping them tells you where a problem is, because changes cluster by region.

Inferior wall
II · III · aVF

Bottom of the heart. Typically supplied by the right coronary artery.

Septal wall
V1 · V2

The wall between the ventricles. Supplied by the LAD.

Anterior wall
V3 · V4

Front of the heart. Also LAD territory.

Lateral wall
I · aVL · V5 · V6

Side wall. Supplied by the circumflex and diagonal branches.

Lead aVR points away from the heart and is often used as a quality and direction check rather than a regional view.
Remember

Don't read an EKG all at once. Walk it in order — rate, rhythm, axis, intervals, morphology, ST/T — and the pattern reveals itself.
